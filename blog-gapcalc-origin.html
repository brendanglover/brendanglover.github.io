I tried to build a welding table.
It got complicated.

What started as a simple beginner project turned into a broken machine, a torch with the wrong fitting, and a free web app now used across 11 countries — none of which I planned, and none of which is finished. Here's an honest account of where things actually stand.

I've owned a MIG welder for years. It sat in the garage doing very little while I told myself I'd "get back into it." That's a familiar story for anyone with a garage and good intentions. Last year I finally decided to stop making excuses and build something — a proper welding table. Simple flat bars spaced evenly across a steel frame. A beginner project. How hard could it be.

The answer, it turns out, is: harder than the welding itself.

The first problem wasn't the welding

Before I could strike a single arc, I had to figure out what I actually needed to buy and cut. I knew the frame dimensions. I knew roughly what I wanted the top to look like. But I had no idea how many flat bars to order, how to space them evenly, what the offcuts would look like, or how much the whole thing would weigh — which matters when you're hauling steel in a family car.

I went looking for a calculator. Something that would take my frame dimensions and bar width and just tell me the numbers. I found plenty of generic spacing tools online, none of which worked the way fabricators actually think about the problem. Most assumed gaps at the edges. Most ignored kerf. None gave me a cut list I could take to the supplier.

So I built one myself. Not because I'm a developer — I'm not — but because I was frustrated enough, and I'd recently learned that "I can't code" is a much smaller obstacle than it used to be.

That first calculator — a gap calculator for spacing flat bars evenly across a frame — became GapCalc. It launched with one tool. It now has eleven. Each one exists because I hit another wall while planning the same project.

Then the machine broke

With my materials calculated and my cut list sorted, I went to actually weld something. That's when I discovered my torch was in a bad way — burnt head assembly, fouled liner, the kind of damage that happens when a machine sits unused and then gets mistreated. Beyond repair.

So I ordered a replacement. The replacement arrived with the wrong pin layout for my machine socket. Back it goes. I've since ordered the correct torch through a proper supplier, and I'm now waiting on a few prep tools before I can strip and prep the frame properly.

The welding table frame is sitting in my garage right now, fully cut, waiting. It has been waiting for a while. I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

What kept moving while everything else stalled

Here's the thing about a project that breaks down at every step: the learning doesn't stop. If anything, the obstacles accelerated it.

Trying to fix the torch taught me more about how my machine actually works than years of occasional use ever did. Understanding the pin layout mismatch meant reading wiring diagrams. Diagnosing the liner meant understanding wire feed mechanics. Things I'd never have looked into if the torch had just worked.

The same thing happened with GapCalc. Every calculator I built forced me to understand the underlying problem properly before I could write a single line. The angle cut calculator required me to actually understand trigonometry well enough to explain it to someone else. The rod estimator meant researching weld deposition rates, rod classifications, joint types, positions. I know more about stick welding now than I did before I started building a MIG calculator.

None of that was the plan. All of it was useful.

The part I didn't expect

Within 28 days of going live with no advertising and no following, GapCalc had visitors from 11 countries. People I'll never meet, planning projects I'll never see, using a tool I built because I couldn't find what I needed myself.

That's not a brag. It's more of a reminder — to myself as much as anyone — that the thing you build to solve your own problem is often the thing someone else needed too. The frustration that makes you build something is usually widely shared. You're rarely as alone in it as you feel.

I'm still fixing the machine. I'm still building GapCalc — there's a MIG settings guide, a job costing tool, a job timer, and more features on the roadmap. The welding table will get built. I don't know exactly when. What I do know is that I've learned more from this being messy and slow than I would have from it going smoothly.

You don't need a complete workshop, a full set of tools, or a clear plan from the start. You need to begin, stay honest about where you are, and keep moving — even when what you're moving is just the next small piece of the puzzle.

If you're in a similar place — garage full of half-finished ideas, a project that's turned into three projects, feeling behind on everything you said you'd do — you're not doing it wrong. That's just what building things actually looks like.

The table will get done. Updates to follow.
